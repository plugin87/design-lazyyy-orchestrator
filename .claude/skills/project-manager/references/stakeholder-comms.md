## 1. Principles

- **Answer first.** Most readers stop after two lines. Put the status and the
  ask at the top; detail below for those who want it.
- **No surprises.** Anyone affected hears it from you before they read it in a
  report. A pre-warning phone call costs five minutes and preserves the
  relationship.
- **Separate fact from forecast**, and label which is which.
- **Every risk and action has a named owner and a date.**
- **Consistent cadence beats detailed but sporadic.** People calibrate on
  rhythm; a missed update reads as a problem being hidden.
- **Write for the reader's decision**, not to demonstrate effort. Executives
  want status, risk, and what you need from them. Teams want detail and
  sequence. Clients want dates, impact on them, and confidence.

## 2. RAG status with real definitions

Define these at kickoff and apply them mechanically. Undefined RAG is
theatre — everything stays green until the week it goes red.

- **Green** — on track for scope, date, and budget with current plan and no
  intervention needed.
- **Amber** — at risk. Will miss something without a specific intervention,
  and that intervention is named. Amber is a request, not a confession.
- **Red** — will miss scope, date, or budget. A decision is required now.

Two rules that keep it honest: Amber must be usable early (a project that
jumps green → red has a reporting failure, not just a delivery failure), and
status can only be changed by evidence, not by optimism about the coming week.

## 3. Weekly status update

```
Subject: [Project] — Week of [date] — [GREEN/AMBER/RED]

Status: [Green/Amber/Red]. [One sentence: on track, or what's at risk and
what's being done.]

Shipped this week
- [outcome, not activity]
- [outcome]

Next week
- [outcome]
- [outcome]

Risks & blockers
| Item | Impact | Owner | By when |
|---|---|---|---|
| | | | |

Decisions needed from you
- [decision] — needed by [date] — [what happens if it's late]

Timeline: [milestone] on track for [date] (forecast range [x]–[y]).
```

Keep it to one screen. If it doesn't fit, the detail belongs in a linked
document. "Shipped" should be outcomes — "customers can now pay with saved
cards" — not "worked on payments."

## 4. Escalation

```
Subject: [Project] — decision needed by [date] — [one-line summary]

What's happened
[Two or three sentences. Facts only, no blame.]

Impact
[Dates, cost, scope, or quality — quantified.]

What we've already done
[Shows this isn't being handed over prematurely.]

Options
1. [Option] — [tradeoff, cost, new date]
2. [Option] — [tradeoff, cost, new date]
3. [Option] — [tradeoff, cost, new date]

Recommendation
[Which and why, in two sentences.]

What I need
[The specific decision, from whom, by when, and the consequence of delay.]
```

Never escalate without options. And escalate to the person who can decide —
routing through three layers of "just to keep you informed" wastes the window
you're escalating to preserve.

## 5. Delivering bad news

Sequence matters more than wording:

1. Tell the most affected person first, directly, before the group.
2. Lead with the news, not the context. "We're going to miss the 12th" comes
   first; the reasons come second. Burying the headline reads as evasion and
   the reader will hunt for it anyway.
3. State the new commitment with your confidence level.
4. Give the cause plainly, without blame and without excessive apology.
   Excessive apology shifts the conversation to managing your feelings.
5. Say what changes so it doesn't recur.
6. Give the options and your recommendation.

What to avoid: passive voice hiding who did what, burying it in paragraph four,
optimistic recovery promises you can't support, and — worst — a second slip
after the recovery plan. If you're unsure of the new date, quote the
pessimistic one.

## 6. Client / external update

Clients need three things: are we on track, what do you need from me, and what
does it cost. Structure:

```
Where we are: [milestone] complete; [milestone] in progress, [x]% through.
On track for [date] / at risk because [reason].

What we need from you: [item] by [date] — without it, [consequence].

Changes since last update: [scope changes and their agreed impact on
date/cost, referencing the change request.]

Coming next: [what they'll see and when they'll see it.]
```

Log every scope change with its date/cost impact at the moment it's requested,
not at the end. Undocumented scope creep is the single most common cause of
disputes on client work, and the conversation is easy in week two and brutal in
week ten.

## 7. RACI

For each significant deliverable, name exactly one **Accountable** person.
Multiple accountable people means none.

- **Responsible** — does the work
- **Accountable** — answers for the outcome; one name only
- **Consulted** — input sought before the decision
- **Informed** — told after

Do this at kickoff for the 5–10 things that actually matter, not for every
task. The value is in surfacing the disagreements about who decides — which
always exist, and which otherwise appear in week eight.

## 8. Meeting formats

**Kickoff** — goal and success measures, scope and non-scope, RACI, timeline
and milestones, risks from the pre-mortem, comms cadence and escalation
thresholds, definition of done. Circulate the written version the same day;
the document, not the meeting, is the deliverable.

**Standup** — what changed, what's blocked, what needs a decision. Fifteen
minutes standing. Not a status report to the manager.

**Refinement** — get the top of the backlog to Ready. Timebox per item.

**Retro** — what to keep, what to change; pick one or two changes with an owner
and a date, and review last retro's actions first. Skipping that review is why
most retros stop mattering.

**Steering committee** — decisions only. Send the pre-read 48 hours ahead;
the meeting is for the two or three decisions that need discussion, not a
narration of the report.

---

## 9. Tone calibration

- To executives: brief, quantified, decision-oriented. Assume they read only
  the first paragraph.
- To the team: specific, contextual, honest about pressure. Do not pass
  stakeholder anxiety through unfiltered — translate it into what actually
  changes for them.
- To clients: warm, precise, and explicit about the boundary between what's
  included and what's a change.
- Under pressure: shorter sentences, more numbers, fewer adjectives. Calm
  reads as competence; elaborate reassurance reads as concealment.
